Overview

This project analyses a NASA exoplanet catalogue [6] of 6,150 planets (31 columns). It follows a standard data analytics workflow: data checks and preprocessing, exploratory analysis, hypothesis testing, multiple linear regression and KMeans clustering. It adds a grouping of planets by host-star type, a temperature analysis, and a habitable-zone study that compares the dataset’s own flag with a zone computed from each star’s luminosity.

Key findings

  • Red dwarfs host smaller planets. Median radius is 1.9 Earth radii for M-type hosts against 2.8 for Sun-like G stars, and 52% of M-dwarf planets are under 2 Earth radii (27% for G).
  • Newer discoveries are smaller. Planets found from 2015 onwards have a geometric mean radius of 3.53 Earth radii against 4.56 before, which reflects the Kepler and TESS surveys.
  • The dataset’s habitable-zone flag is generous. It marks 403 planets (equilibrium temperature 200–400 K). The luminosity-based zone [5] gives 294 (optimistic) or 172 (conservative); only 109 planets are in both, and 286 flagged planets are too hot.
  • Small habitable-zone planets favour red dwarfs. 14.2% of small M-dwarf planets lie in the zone, against 0.4% for G stars.
  • Giants prefer metal-rich stars. Mean host [Fe/H] is +0.071 for giants and −0.028 for rocky planets, in line with the planet–metallicity trend in [1] and [2].
  • Top candidates by Earth Similarity Index: Wolf 1069 b, TRAPPIST-1 e and TOI-700 d.

Limitations

The catalogue reflects what surveys can detect, so counts per star type are not true occurrence rates. Mass is present for 99.5% of planets, so many values are probably estimates from radius. Being in the habitable zone does not mean a planet is habitable.
